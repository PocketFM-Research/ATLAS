=== SCENE 1 ===
The week after the funeral, the Serrano row house held its breath under a film of late-morning light, the kind that showed every place Inez had stopped dusting in her last months. Asha had set her bag down in the library before she'd even taken off her coat; the probate calendar in her head was loud enough that small talk felt like a waste of air. She started with the shelves Inez was known to favor, lifting books in pairs and checking behind the leather Audubons, then knelt to test the underside of the partner's desk for a drawer that didn't match the others. From the dining room came the soft, deliberate sound of Rosa humming, a tune Asha half-recognized from the wake, and the careful drag of a cloth across the mahogany sideboard. When Asha looked up, Rosa was bent close to the lower panel, polishing a seam in the wood that wouldn't have needed polishing if it had only ever been a seam. Neither of them said anything about it, but Asha registered the angle of Rosa's hand and stayed where she was, letting the woman work.

Asha rose from the partner's desk and crossed into the dining room without hurry, stopping at a polite distance from the sideboard so Rosa could choose whether to look up. "Will you show me which part needs the most work?" she asked, keeping her voice low enough that the question sounded like ordinary curiosity. Rosa's humming thinned and stopped; she didn't answer, but her cloth slowed and then bore down on a small recessed bead in the lower panel, working it in tight circles a beat longer than any polishing required. When her thumb pressed the bead flat against the grain, Asha heard the faint, dry click of something giving inside the wood, and Rosa lifted her hand away as if she had only been tidying, eyes still on the cloth. Neither of them spoke into the space the sound had opened, but Asha understood, with a small cold drop in her stomach, that Rosa had been waiting all morning to be asked.

Asha closed the distance to the sideboard and laid two fingers along the bead Rosa had worked, feeling the give where the wood had unlocked itself. The panel swung out a finger's width under her hand, releasing a thin smell of old cedar and paper, and she had just begun to ease it open when Daniel's step sounded in the hall. "What is taking so long in here?" he said, coming through the doorway with his coat still on, his eyes going first to Rosa and the cloth in her hand, then to Asha crouched at his mother's furniture. Asha let her fingers drift from the panel without closing it fully, resting her palm flat on the polished top as if she had only been steadying herself, and Rosa resumed her slow circles on a clean stretch of wood. "The buyers want a walkthrough Thursday," Daniel went on, more to the room than to either of them, and Asha understood that whatever was inside the compartment would have to be taken out with him standing there or not taken out at all today.

Rosa moved before Asha did, stepping with the cloth still in her hand into the line between Daniel and the sideboard, her shoulder catching the edge of the open panel so that her body covered the gap. "There's a water mark under the leaf, Daniel, I want to ask you about it before Thursday," she said, lifting the cloth slightly as if to show evidence, and on the word Thursday she let her eyes flick once to Asha, a small steadying look that asked for the next move. Asha rose from her crouch in the same beat, settling her weight in front of the panel and sliding her hand back along the polished top until her fingers rested over the seam. "While we're all here," she said to Daniel, keeping her voice in the register she used for clients she did not yet trust, "did your mother mention any updates to the property deeds in the last few months, anything she wanted changed before the sale went forward?" Daniel's coat was still buttoned; he looked from Rosa's cloth to Asha's hand and did not answer at once, and in that pause Asha understood that the question itself had told him something she had not meant to give away yet.

Rosa moved a half step toward Daniel and lifted the cloth so it pointed at a pale ring on the leaf of the sideboard, her shoulder still angled across the open seam. "Here, under the curve, do you see it," she said, and the question pulled Daniel's gaze down to the wood she wanted him to see, away from the panel at Asha's hip. Under cover of that small redirection Asha let her palm slide back along the polished top, fingers slipping into the gap, and eased the panel open another finger's width until she felt the dry edge of folded paper inside. She drew the pages out flat against her sleeve and tucked them into the inside pocket of her jacket in one slow motion, keeping her face turned toward Daniel as if she were only waiting for his answer about the deeds. When he finally looked up from the watermark his eyes went to her hand on the wood and not to her pocket, and she understood she had perhaps a few hours before he thought back through the morning and realized what he had not seen.

Rosa did not give the moment back to Daniel. She stepped in nearer the leaf and tapped the pale ring with one fingertip, asking him if he remembered who had left it, and the question landed differently than the cloth had a minute before, because it asked him to account for a night in his mother's house rather than a flaw in her wood. Daniel's mouth moved before any answer came; his eyes went past Rosa to the curve she was pointing at and stayed there a beat too long. Asha used that beat to lift her hand off the polished top, smooth as if she had only been leaning, and stepped clear of the sideboard with the folded pages a small, definite weight inside her jacket. "Since you're here," she said to Daniel in her client voice, "let's go through the deed list together in the study, before Thursday gets any closer," and she was already turning toward the hall, leaving Rosa and the watermark behind her as cover.

In the study Asha laid the deed list out across the desk, smoothing it flat with the side of her hand, and asked Daniel to walk her through the most recent amendments before Thursday, particularly anything touching the house itself. He had followed her in a beat behind, and at the threshold he stopped, glanced back down the hall toward the dining room sideboard, and held there a moment before crossing to the chair across from her. He did not sit at once. His eyes moved over the list and back to her face, and when he asked which amendments she meant his voice came a half-step slower than it had over the watermark, as if he were already counting backward through the morning to find the place where something had shifted without his noticing.

Asha drew the deed list a fraction closer to her side of the desk and asked him, in the same level voice she had used over the watermark, to put his finger on the entry for the house itself. Daniel lowered himself into the chair without quite settling into it, his weight still forward, and his eyes traveled the page without landing. "Which ones specifically?" he asked, and his glance went briefly to the open study door and the length of hall beyond it before returning to the paper. He did not look at her pocket, or at her hand, but his attention had begun to circle something it had not yet named, and Asha let the silence sit a beat longer than was comfortable before she tapped the line for the title and waited for him to read it aloud.

Asha kept her eyes on Daniel and asked him, in the same even register, why this particular line wanted so much specificity from him before Thursday; she would rather, she said, hear the title read out than paraphrase it for him. Daniel's finger came down on the line a moment later than it should have, and he read the entry aloud in a careful, half-lawyered voice, the words about the house and its conveyance to him sounding less settled in his mouth than they did on the page. When he finished he did not lift his finger at once. Instead he asked her why this amendment, of all of them, needed walking through now, and the question carried the small, defensive lift of someone trying to learn what she already knew without admitting he wanted to. Asha let the deed list stay where it was between them and did not answer immediately, and the folded pages inside her jacket felt, against her ribs, like a thing she had not yet decided when to put on the desk.

Asha let the question of why-this-line sit on the desk a moment longer, and then, without explanation, she reached into her jacket and drew out the folded pages. She laid them on top of the deed list, smoothed the crease once with her thumb, and slid them across the wood until they stopped just short of Daniel's hand. He withdrew his finger from the title line as if the paper had warmed under it, leaned back a fraction in the chair he had never quite settled into, and looked at the new pages without yet touching them. "Is there some other reason we're focusing on this today?" he asked, and the question came out lighter than it wanted to be, the lawyering thinned away from it. Asha did not answer; she watched his eyes track the first visible line of their mother's handwriting and saw the small, specific stillness of a man recognizing something he had hoped was not in the house.

Asha leaned forward a fraction over the desk, kept her eyes on his, and asked in the same quiet register whether the hand on the page looked familiar to him. Daniel's fingers found the edge of the folded sheet before he seemed to decide they would, and he drew the pages a small distance toward himself, not enough to claim them, only enough to angle the writing into his own light. His glance went once to the open study door and the empty hall, then back down, and the careful lawyering he had brought into the room earlier was no longer in his shoulders. He did not answer her question. The silence that took its place was the kind that admitted the handwriting before his mouth was willing to.

Asha did not break the silence; she watched Daniel's thumb and forefinger close on the edges of the folded sheet and asked, in the same low register she had used for the title line, when he had last seen this. He drew the pages the rest of the way across the wood then, claiming them in a single unhurried motion that tried to look like nothing, and when he raised his face to her he had assembled a thin smile that did not reach the rest of him. Behind him, Rosa had come as far as the study doorway with the tea tray and stopped there, not yet stepping in, her eyes resting on Daniel's hands and the paper under them long enough that Asha registered the pause without turning her head. Daniel felt it; his shoulders set against the doorway without his looking around, and the smile tightened by a degree he could not have hidden if he had tried. Rosa carried the tray the rest of the way to the side table and set it down quietly, and the question Asha had asked stayed in the room, unanswered, between the three of them.

Asha let her gaze travel from Daniel's tight, assembled smile to the doorway where Rosa had set the tray, and she asked, in the same low register she had been using all afternoon, whether Rosa recognized the hand. The question landed before Rosa had straightened from the side table, and Daniel moved before Rosa could answer. He folded the pages back along their existing crease with the deliberate care of a man not wanting to be seen hurrying, slid them across the wood toward Asha until they sat closer to her side than to his, and asked why she was bringing this up now. The cup Rosa had been about to lift stayed on the tray. Asha did not reach for the pages; she let them rest between her hand and his, and noted that he had not looked at Rosa once since the question had been put to her.

Asha drew the folded sheet back across the desk with two fingers, slow enough that the gesture read less as taking than as ending the question of who held it, and set it on her own side of the wood. Without raising her voice she asked Rosa when she had last seen these. Rosa lifted the cup she had set down a moment before, took a small sip the way she might have at any other afternoon in this house, and said, almost to the tray, that the ink looked fresh. Her eyes went, only once and only briefly, to Daniel's hand where the knuckles had gone white against the edge of the desk, and then back to Asha. Daniel did not speak; the smile he had assembled earlier had thinned to something he was no longer maintaining, and the room held the three of them in the new arrangement without anyone moving to soften it.

Asha drew the sheet flat with the heel of her hand, smoothing the long crease open against the desk so the lower half of the page lay exposed between them, and asked Rosa, without looking up, which part of the ink she had meant. Rosa did not answer at once; her eyes went to the paragraph nearest the signature line, the one that named the house, and stayed there long enough to be an answer of a kind. Daniel let go of the desk edge then, the white pressure leaving his knuckles in stages, and stood, smoothing the front of his jacket as if the gesture might carry the room with him. He said, in a voice gone careful around its own evenness, that she could leave the documents on the desk, he would be back in a moment, and walked past Rosa to the doorway without turning his head. Asha kept her hand where it was on the page and let him go, and the tray on the side table, the cup Rosa had set down, and the unsmoothed half of the fold all stayed exactly where they were.

=== SCENE 2 ===
After Daniel's footsteps had faded down the hall, Asha did not lift her hand from the page. Rosa stayed where she was beside the tray, the cup still set down, and the two of them held the study between them in a silence that no longer needed to perform itself for him. The codicil question had not resolved into proof; what sat on the desk was a folded sheet whose ink Rosa had quietly named fresh, and whose paragraph naming the house had drawn her eyes the way water finds a low place. Somewhere above them a floorboard shifted, and Asha remembered that Lucia was still in the house, still holding back whatever she had been holding back, and that Daniel would not stay out of this room for long.

Asha leaned a few inches across the desk, her hand still flat on the page, and asked Rosa under her breath where Lucia was. Rosa did not move closer, but she set her fingertips lightly on the edge of the tray as if to steady it, glanced once at the empty doorway, and said, almost without sound, that the girl was upstairs and that they had very little time. Asha slid the folded sheet a few inches toward her own side of the desk, away from the door, and let her palm rest over the paragraph that named the house. Neither of them spoke about what Lucia had been keeping; it was understood now, between the tray and the desk, that whatever it was would have to come down the stairs in the next few minutes or not at all.

Asha caught Rosa's eye across the desk and said, barely above breath, to get her down here now, sliding the folded sheet another few inches toward her own ribs so the paragraph naming the house sat hidden under her cuff and the angle of her body. Rosa did not step away from the tray at once; she leaned a fraction toward Asha instead, fingertips still on the lacquer rim, and murmured that the service stairs were faster, with one sharp glance toward the hall where Daniel's footsteps had gone. The suggestion landed as a small correction, the kind a person made who had been counting the house's shortcuts for thirty years, and Asha understood without answering that Rosa had already mapped the route before being asked. Rosa lifted her hand from the tray then, smoothed her apron once, and moved toward the inner doorway that gave onto the back of the kitchen, leaving the cup where she had set it as if she meant to come back to it. Asha kept her palm flat on the page and listened, past the ordinary creaks of the house, for two sets of footsteps coming down instead of one going up.

As Rosa slipped through the inner doorway toward the back stairs, Asha lifted her hand from the corner of the page, glanced once at the empty hall where Daniel had gone, and slid the folded sheet the rest of the way under her cuff so that the paragraph naming the house lay against the inside of her wrist. The study felt larger without Rosa in it; the cup on the tray had stopped steaming, and the small sounds of the house, a radiator tick, the give of a board overhead, arranged themselves into a count Asha did not want to be keeping. Then there were the footsteps she had been listening for, two sets, light and quick on the service treads at the back of the kitchen, Rosa's measured and Lucia's a half-beat behind, and Asha understood without seeing them that Lucia was coming down with one hand pressed flat to her chest over whatever she had carried upstairs and not put down since. Asha kept her wrist where it was on the desk and did not turn toward the inner door yet, because turning too soon would make the moment into something Lucia could still refuse.

Rosa came through the inner door first, her hand at the small of Lucia's back, steering her past the jamb the way she might have guided her through a hot kitchen, and the girl crossed into the study with one hand pressed flat against her chest where the cloth of her blouse showed the corner of something folded underneath. Asha did not lift her wrist from the page yet; she only turned her head, slowly, until she had Lucia's eyes, and asked under her breath what she had brought them. Lucia stopped a pace inside the room, her fingers tightening over the shape against her sternum, and looked at Asha and then at the cooling cup on the tray as if deciding which of the two adults in the room she was answering. Rosa kept her hand where it was on Lucia's shoulder and said nothing, which was its own kind of pressure, and the radiator ticked once in the corner while none of them moved toward the desk.

Asha lifted her wrist from the page, slowly, and turned her palm up on the desk between them, an open hand without reach in it, the folded sheet still tucked at her cuff so Lucia could see she was being asked and not stripped. Rosa felt the girl hesitate under her hand and pressed her fingers once at the back of Lucia's shoulder, the small steady pressure of someone who had walked her through worse rooms, and Lucia took the half-step forward almost without meaning to, her other hand still flattened over the cloth of her blouse. For a moment she only stood there at the edge of the desk, looking at Asha's open palm and then at the cooling cup beside it, and Asha did not close her fingers or speak. Then Lucia drew the folded paper out from under her blouse, the edge of it warm and creased into the shape of her ribs, and set it down on the desk beside Asha's wrist without letting go all at once, her fingertips resting on the fold a breath longer before she lifted them away.

Asha drew the folded sheet from her cuff and laid it flat beside Lucia's, the two papers almost touching at their long edges, and kept her palms open on the desk where both women could see them. "Now," she said, low enough that the radiator's tick stayed audible under it, "let us see if they match." Lucia took another half-step in, close enough now that her hip nearly grazed the desk, and her eyes moved from the paragraph naming the house to the looser hand on her own page and back, her fingers still half-curled as if she had not entirely let the second sheet go. Rosa kept her place a pace behind, hand fallen from Lucia's shoulder but not far, and none of them spoke yet about which of the two hands belonged to the woman they had buried.

Lucia drew the paper open the rest of the way, smoothing the last fold with the side of her thumb, though her fingertips stayed on the crease a beat longer than they needed to before she let her hand fall to the edge of the desk. Asha set her index finger between the two sheets and slid them flush at the long edge, lining the margins until the paragraphs sat parallel under the lamp, then lifted her eyes to Lucia's and held them. Rosa stepped in from behind and bent at the waist over the desk, close enough now that Asha could smell the kitchen on her sleeve, her gaze moving down the looser hand on Lucia's page and across to the firmer one that named the house. None of them spoke for a moment; the radiator ticked again, and Lucia's breathing evened out against the quiet, as if Rosa's shoulder near her own had given her permission to look directly at what she had been carrying.

Asha drew the two sheets together until they overlapped, edge to edge, lining the long downstrokes of the firmer hand against the looser ones beneath until the paragraphs sat one atop the other under the lamp. She lifted her eyes from the paper and held Lucia's. "Which one is hers?" she asked, no louder than the radiator's tick. Lucia's fingertip went to the page before her mouth did, tracing the gap where the two scripts diverged in the slant of an l, the tail of a y, the way the firmer hand pressed the pen at the end of each line as if someone wanted to be sure of being read. Her finger stopped on the looser stroke, and she looked sideways at Rosa before she answered, waiting to see whether Rosa would let her say it.

Rosa's eyes moved from the diverging script to Lucia's fingertip where it rested on the looser stroke, and she gave a single slow nod, glancing once at Asha and once back, asking nothing and refusing nothing. Lucia drew a breath that caught at the top, then let it out against the lamplight. "The looser one," she said, almost under the radiator's tick, "that was my mother's." Her finger did not lift from the page after she said it; it pressed a little, as if to keep the words from sliding off. Asha kept her own hands open on the desk and did not move them, letting the sentence sit there between the two sheets while somewhere down the hall a door had not yet opened again.

Asha eased the overlapping sheets a quarter inch apart, just enough to set the firmer l beside the looser one under the lamp, and lifted her eyes to Lucia's. "And this one?" she asked, the question barely above the radiator. Lucia's gaze rose slowly from the page to meet hers, holding there with something tight behind the eyes, as though deciding whether naming the second hand would oblige her to say more than she had come prepared to say. Rosa shifted in beside her and laid a hand low against Lucia's back, steadying her without pressing, then cut a quick look toward the hall door, where no footstep had yet sounded but soon would. Lucia did not answer at once; her finger stayed on her mother's stroke, and the firmer line waited under the light for her to give it a name.

Asha leaned in until her shoulder almost touched the lamp's brass arm and dropped her voice below the radiator's hum. "Who wrote the firmer one?" she asked. Rosa's hand pressed once at the small of Lucia's back, a brief steady push, and she breathed the words "tell her" so quietly that they barely carried, her eyes cutting toward the closed hall door before returning to the page. Lucia swallowed, glanced down at where Rosa's hand still rested, and let her finger slide a fraction across the paper from the looser stroke to the firmer one. "This one," she said, and the word came out thinned, "this was my father's." Her finger stayed there after, as if the admission needed weighting down too, and Asha did not move, letting the second name settle on the desk beside the first while the hallway beyond the door kept its silence a little longer.

Rosa's eyes cut once more to the closed hall door and came back, and her hand moved from the small of Lucia's back to her shoulder, where it closed in a brief, firm squeeze before tipping her almost imperceptibly toward Asha. Lucia took the cue without lifting her head; her fingers slid under the edge of the firmer sheet and drew it forward across the blotter, nudging the looser one aside until both her parents' strokes lay clear in the lamplight. She set her fingertip back on the divergence and pushed the page another inch toward Asha, so that the discrepancy sat squarely under Asha's eyes rather than her own. "Look," she said, and the word was steadier than the ones before it, though she did not take her hand away. The radiator ticked twice into the quiet that followed, and none of them mistook it for time they still had.

Asha bent closer until her shoulder grazed the lamp's brass arm, her eye fixed on the place where the two strokes ran parallel without ever quite touching. "Then why do these two lines never actually meet?" she asked, barely above a whisper. Lucia did not answer; instead her free hand went to the pocket of her cardigan and came out with a folded sheet, creased soft at the edges from being carried too long, and she laid it over the divergent strokes on the blotter and slid her fingers back as if the paper might burn them. It was a short letter in the firmer hand, dated two months before her mother's death, naming Rosa as the one to whom the house should pass for staying. For a moment none of them spoke; the radiator ticked, and Lucia kept her eyes on the desk rather than on either woman beside her, as though she had set down something she was not sure she would be allowed to pick back up.

Asha's fingertips found the soft crease of the folded letter and slid it down over the divergent strokes until the firmer hand on Lucia's letter lay directly above the firmer hand on the desk page, the two inked lines stacked now in a single narrow band of lamplight. Lucia did not pull back; her own fingertips lingered at the crease alongside Asha's, then pushed the paper another fraction forward, a small ceding gesture, before her eyes flicked once to the closed hall door and returned. Beyond that door, Daniel had stopped short of the handle; the floorboard outside the study took his weight and held it, and he stood listening for the shape of voices he could not quite make out. Inside, none of them spoke; the radiator ticked, and the alignment of the two firmer hands sat under the lamp waiting to be read as either the same writer twice or a careful imitation of her.

Asha drew the folded letter slowly off the blotter, uncovering the firmer hand on the desk page alone again under the lamp, and lifted her eyes to Lucia's. "Is this the last piece?" she whispered, the question pitched low enough that it barely cleared the brass shade. Lucia's fingertips slid back from the crease as if released, and her gaze went once to the closed hall door before it returned to Asha; "He knows," she said, two words no louder than the radiator's tick. On the other side of that door Daniel had set his ear close to the wood, breath held, trying to catch the shape of what was being said in the cadence of their voices, and the floorboard under his weight gave the smallest creak that none of them inside could be sure they had heard.

The radiator's tick covered the small creak in the hall, but only barely; Lucia's two words sat between them like a third document on the desk, and Asha understood that the window she had counted on—Daniel safely elsewhere, the three of them deciding in private what to do with the page under the lamp—had already closed without her noticing it shut. Rosa drew a half step back from the desk and laid her palm flat on the blotter beside the letter, a steadying gesture that was also a claim, and Lucia's hand went again to the empty pocket of her cardigan as if checking for something she had already given away. Beyond the door, the floorboard settled under a weight that did not retreat. Whatever they chose now would have to be chosen with him listening, and the choice was no longer whether to compare the two hands in secret but whether to open the door themselves and put the aligned pages, the letter, and Rosa's memory in front of Daniel before the closing meeting at the attorney's office in the morning made any private finding too late to matter.

Asha did not look up at Rosa or Lucia for permission. She gathered the desk page first, slid Lucia's letter exactly on top of it the way they had aligned them under the lamp, and folded the small stack into the inner sleeve of her briefcase along the long edge so nothing would crease. Asha lifted the briefcase from the desk by its handle rather than its strap, so the buckles would not knock, and tipped her chin once toward the narrow door at the back of the study that gave onto the service stair. === SCENE 4 ===
The service stair let them down past the pantry and out through the kitchen garden door, where the cold air took the warmth out of Asha's collar and the briefcase stayed pressed against her ribs as if breath alone could keep the seams from giving. Rosa locked the garden gate behind them with the key she had carried for thirty years and did not put back on the hook. Lucia's car was at the curb two houses down, unlit, and they reached it without anyone calling from the front steps, but as Asha settled the briefcase between her feet on the passenger floor she understood that getting the pages out of the house was only the first half of the night: by morning the documents had to exist somewhere Daniel could not reach them and somewhere a court would credit, and Lucia and Rosa, who had carried the truth in their bodies for longer than any page had carried it on paper, had to be willing to put their names to what they knew before the closing meeting opened the file at nine.

Rosa caught Lucia's wrist as she reached for the handle, not hard, but with the same steady grip she had once used to walk her up the stairs after the funeral, and she did not let go until Lucia was settled in the driver's seat with the door pulled quietly shut.
